# Evidence

## Michelle (PGR, criminal justice / social science, qualitative, Russell Group Scotland)

### E-01
Source: Michelle
Quote: "I can't say that I've ever benefited from it, so I don't think I've got some experiences that and in terms of my own data, yeah, I think I am a bit cautious about…"
Note: No personal experience of benefiting from open research; caution about own data comes before any experience of the upside.

### E-02
Source: Michelle
Quote: "somebody may really want to interact with my research, but on the consent form it says that you know the data is going to be uploaded to the UK Data service, and therefore that prohibits them from participating in it. So there's kind of like, there's a here-and-now barrier"
Note: Belief that stating deposit on the consent form deters recruitment — sharing is perceived as a cost to the primary study, not just a downstream issue.

### E-03
Source: Michelle
Quote: "it doesn't matter how much metadata that you put with qualitative data you're not there... No amount [of] metadata can provide the context for when it was collected."
Note: Core epistemic objection: context (political landscape, funding, organisational state) can't be captured in metadata; reuse risks misreading.

### E-04
Source: Michelle
Quote: "when you carry out research, it's kind of a production isn't it - with your participants... when you upload it to [the] UK data service or you put it in a repository, that story, that knowledge that you created together. It's gone."
Note: Sees data as co-produced with participants; depositing feels like severing the relationship/partnership. Ethical unease is relational, not procedural.

### E-05
Source: Michelle
Quote: "I think technically, I don't think there's many barriers ethically. Your own positionality, you feel comfortable doing that?"
Note: Explicitly says the technical/procedural barriers are low; the real barrier is the researcher's own positionality/comfort.

### E-06
Source: Michelle
Quote: "the problem would be - is if some just came and said 'well how would it be used?' ... I am 'don't know' - you can't, you can't anticipate that kind of thing."
Note: Can't answer participants' question about future use → consent for unknown future reuse feels hollow.

### E-07
Source: Michelle
Quote: "was it like the interview schedule?... I think that's different... that should be available and we should all be helping each other on that kind of thing - but when it's a participant's personal experience then. Yeah. I think that's different."
Note: Draws a sharp line: instruments/protocols should be open and shared; participants' personal accounts are a different category.

### E-08
Source: Michelle
Quote: "I don't think there's enough information about it... it's not what we got trained on. It's not included in a lot of the information that they have, it's just kind of like new."
Note: Lack of training / guidance on open qual data; feels new and unfamiliar.

### E-09
Source: Michelle
Quote: "has it come from the idea, from like a quant space thing... it seems better. Seems the right way to do it. I don't know if that's where you think that it's arisen, from whether that's something that we're comfortable with as researchers as well."
Note: Suspicion that open data norms are imported from quantitative science and treated as a quality marker without fitting qualitative epistemology.

### E-10
Source: Michelle
Quote: "the one that's most similar to mine was conducted back in 1997... the organisation that I'm looking at has changed so drastically... I'd like some guidance - other than just like really basic metadata, just have that really rich kind of setting about - this is what happened"
Note: Would consider reusing others' data if it came with rich contextual narrative, not just metadata. Reuse conditional on context.

### E-11
Source: Michelle
Quote: "the people who support you is likely data management plans, I think it is a library services, they are just absolutely fantastic... So helpful and so insightful."
Note: Library/RDM support highly valued; DMP is the touchpoint where open data first appears for PGRs.

### E-12
Source: Michelle
Quote: "I think a little bit of flexibility would be nice in the process... 'Read me'. The first page, this is what it was like in 2022 with 10 years of austerity, and covid."
Note: Wants a flexible, narrative-friendly deposit format (a contextual README) rather than a rigid spreadsheet/metadata form.

### E-13
Source: Michelle
Quote: "you possibly feel more comfortable with it if you could have that conversation with a real person to say, Okay, you might not find this sensitive, but actually, that's because you don't understand the context of it... I feel at the moment, we're just kind of like a little machines and it gets sent off into the ether"
Note: Wants a human conversation with the repository about access-level classification; current process feels impersonal.

### E-14
Source: Michelle
Quote: "It's not [being] precious about your data and not wanting to share it. It's about sharing it responsibly."
Note: Self-framing: not opposed to sharing, opposed to careless sharing. Also: "I'd hate somebody to pick it up in five years time and say, Well, that practitioner didn't do that" — fear of participants being judged out of context.

## David (early-career, healthcare/self-tracking in running, information science, qualitative, audio-visual)

### E-15
Source: David
Quote: "I do try and build rigour into my research. But that tends to be more through transparency."
Note: Rigour in interpretive work is reframed as transparency of process rather than reproducibility of result — openness is a rigour strategy.

### E-16
Source: David
Quote: "people want things up like their faces blurred out... I can see the benefits that if I put this in the UKDA and put an embargo on it for 10 or 15 years... but it's not going to be that useful in the interim"
Note: Audio-visual/360 data is the hard case; anonymisation techniques (blurring, long embargoes) hollow out the reuse value. Data-type-specific barrier.

### E-17
Source: David
Quote: "The messy mapping, the relational mapping, and the positional maps. I keep those, and because I want to show my workings out... the idea there, is to deposit all that kind of stuff as well"
Note: Wants to share analytical artefacts (maps, memos) — the "workings out" — as much as raw data. Openness of analysis distinct from openness of data.

### E-18
Source: David
Quote: "I think it's easier to share transcripts... I think that's fine, again, like my analysis, that's fine."
Note: Same instrument/analysis vs. personal data gradient as Michelle, but drawn at a different point: transcripts are on the "fine" side for him.

### E-19
Source: David
Quote: "I'm firmly aware that somebody would interpret the data that I collected completely, and come up with different... results... but it's not really my research"
Note: Reinterpretation by others is accepted as normal, even valued — direct contrast with Michelle's fear of misreading.

### E-20
Source: David
Quote: "there is a bit of a barrier in who do I go to?... it's a bit like, where do I even start? I think the same applies to UKDA as well... but then there's that question of money, of the funding that's needed to deposit it."
Note: Two barriers: not knowing who to ask (navigational), and cost of deposit (esp. AV data at UKDA).

### E-21
Source: David
Quote: "I don't really want to delete the data, but then, you know, that's what I told the participants I would do - if that's what they've signed for."
Note: Consent locks in destruction; regrets promising deletion. Consent is a one-way ratchet toward closed.

### E-22
Source: David
Quote: "if that's on a research grant... I don't always think that's the right spirit... if there is a bigger open research culture is actually fostered, then there's going to be less resistance"
Note: Mandates produce compliance not culture; prefers culture change via champions/networks. Sceptical of funder-mandate as the lever.

### E-23
Source: David
Quote: "as an early career researcher, qualitative researcher - you feel the need to have to keep proving yourself a bit... I just feel enough to keep justifying myself a bit more and really over-emphasizing how I got to the final"
Note: Status anxiety of qualitative ECR relative to quant/data science; openness partly a defensive move to earn credibility.

### E-24
Source: David
Quote: "I use a series of... step by step pictures. Really force where the data goes, what happens to it, like decision points"
Note: Concrete workaround: visual data-flow diagrams in participant information to make future use legible. Front-loads consent effort.

### E-25
Source: David
Quote: "I give them some three options... I give them the completely open option... what I tend to find happens is they meet in the middle, so I call it tailored."
Note: Tiered/menu consent (closed / tailored / open), recorded per participant. Practical mechanism for granular sharing.

### E-26
Source: David
Quote: "I work from a position of everything's open then kind of get negotiated down because it's not in my gift to tell the participant 'this should be open'... if you start from the position of closed, you can't really move."
Note: Default-open-then-negotiate stance; anchoring effect of the starting position. Participant, not researcher, decides.

### E-27
Source: David
Quote: "I'd be a lot less likely to use something like Internet Archive... if it's UKDA, or institutional repository of data sets, that, yes certainly"
Note: Repository trust/provenance is a condition of reuse.

### E-28
Source: David
Quote: "they're not entirely open, because you need a password to start. To me, that gives the data a bit of reassurance that not everybody's looking at it."
Note: Even the open-research enthusiast wants gated access as reassurance — "open" means findable + controlled, not public.

### E-29
Source: David
Quote: "it's all at the start of the project. It's not a retrospective thing, it's at the start. And that sets in train everything else"
Note: DMP at project conception is where openness is decided; can't be retrofitted.

### E-30
Source: David
Quote: "there was one dataset from 1976 that seems to be on the system, and it was interviews... I've done like, a data review literature search sort of thing. But as a data. Yeah, I don't go beyond that."
Note: Even a willing reuser finds the existing archive thin/dated; searching for reusable qual data is a box-ticking exercise in practice.

### E-31
Source: David
Quote: "I did a lot of work around... data protection and now GDPR. So, I come to research having that knowledge of how data protection works"
Note: Prior professional info-management background explains his confidence; his practice may not generalise to researchers without it.

# Insights

## I-01 — The transcript is a subset of the evidence; context doesn't travel
Claim: Across disciplines, participants say the thing that makes qualitative data meaningful (political moment, organisational state, sector jargon, tacit knowledge from being there) is not in the transcript and can't be captured in standard metadata, so a secondary reader misreads or reads "sterile".
Evidence: E-03, E-10, E-44, E-52, E-63, E-78
Sources: Michelle, Bruce, Stephen, Penni
Confidence: high
Counter-evidence: E-19, E-35, E-59 — David, Bruce, Stephen simultaneously say a different reading is legitimate and even valuable. The objection is about *missing* context, not about *different* interpretation; the two should not be collapsed. Bruce holds both positions at once (E-35 vs E-44).
Opportunity: A deposit format with a first-class narrative context layer (what was happening, where, why) rather than only structured metadata fields.

## I-02 — Interpretivist epistemology is not the barrier
Claim: Every academic participant with an explicit interpretive/relativist stance says that stance is compatible with, or actively favours, others reinterpreting their data; the barriers they name are practical and ethical, not philosophical.
Evidence: E-19, E-35, E-59, E-61, E-05
Sources: David, Bruce, Stephen, Michelle
Confidence: high
Counter-evidence: E-04, E-14 — Michelle's relational/co-production framing makes deposit feel like severing a partnership, and she fears participants being judged out of context. That's an ethical-relational objection dressed in epistemic language; it's real but it's not "interpretivism forbids sharing". E-66 — Penni doesn't have an articulable epistemology at all.
Opportunity: Stop leading with "is your epistemology compatible?" in guidance and outreach; lead with the practical and relational questions instead.

## I-03 — Open-data norms are perceived as a quantitative import, and that framing itself provokes resistance
Claim: Participants read "open data" as carrying reproducibility/replicability assumptions from quantitative science; they resist the transfer of those quality criteria, and the resistance is triggered by wording as much as substance.
Evidence: E-09, E-34, E-49, E-15, E-23, E-69
Sources: Michelle, Bruce, David, Penni
Confidence: high
Counter-evidence: none found for the perception; note that David and Penni respond by *embracing* transparency as the qual equivalent of rigour rather than rejecting the norm (E-15, E-69), so the same perception produces opposite behaviours.
Opportunity: Reframe open qual data around transparency of process rather than reproducibility of result; audit guidance language for "normal science" framings (Bruce flagged this in the study's own information sheet).

## I-04 — Consent to unknown future reuse is thin, and everyone knows it
Claim: Participants can't answer "how will it be used?", so consent to archive is either not sought (locking data closed), sought as a box that participants can't meaningfully evaluate, or split from consent to research use.
Evidence: E-06, E-02, E-21, E-39, E-40, E-71, E-80
Sources: Michelle, David, Bruce, Penni
Confidence: high
Counter-evidence: E-24, E-25, E-26 — David's visual data-flow diagrams and closed/tailored/open menu are a working answer, but he is one participant with a prior GDPR background (E-31). E-58 — Stephen predates modern consent regimes entirely.
Opportunity: A consent toolkit with visual data-flow and tiered options that separates "use in this study" from "deposit" from "open".

## I-05 — Full anonymity is a fiction; the hard cases are small-N, organisational, and audio-visual
Claim: Participants converge on de-identification rather than anonymity as the achievable target, and identify structural cases where even that fails: elite/stakeholder interviews (one of three people), colleagues in the same organisation, named companies, and faces/voices in AV data.
Evidence: E-36, E-55, E-57, E-68, E-16, E-37
Sources: Bruce, Stephen, Penni, David
Confidence: high
Counter-evidence: E-57 — Stephen notes organisations often accept being recognisable as long as unnamed (the legal trigger is the name), which is a partial opening rather than a counter.
Opportunity: Explicit decision guidance on *which* data types and populations are candidates for deposit at all, and an honest "not shareable, and that's fine" pathway (Bruce E-37 shows opt-out already exists).

## I-06 — Nobody knows where to start, including the experienced
Claim: Awareness of guidance for open qualitative data is close to zero at every career stage and sector; the topic hasn't reached training, journals, or professional bodies, and even those who've looked don't know who to ask.
Evidence: E-08, E-20, E-50, E-60, E-79, E-43
Sources: Michelle, David, Stephen, Penni, Bruce
Confidence: high
Counter-evidence: E-11 — Michelle rates library RDM support as excellent; E-47 — Bruce knows UKDS metadata guidance exists but hasn't used it. So resources exist; discoverability and entry point are the problem.
Opportunity: A single entry point ("start here for qual data") routed through existing trusted touchpoints — DMP review, library RDM, SRA/professional bodies.

## I-07 — Deposit has real, uncosted costs (time, money, metadata)
Claim: De-identification, transcription for strangers, and AV deposit consume significant time and money; where it's not budgeted it becomes a bottleneck or a reason not to deposit; and metadata work is skipped because no one demands it.
Evidence: E-41, E-42, E-20, E-67, E-71, E-43, E-38
Sources: Bruce, David, Penni, Michelle (implicit E-12)
Confidence: high
Counter-evidence: E-42, E-48 — Bruce shows it's manageable when planned and costed from the start; the cost is a planning failure more than an intrinsic barrier.
Opportunity: Costing templates and explicit funder permission to budget archiving effort; treat de-identification as a scheduled work package, not an afterthought.

## I-08 — Openness is decided at project conception and can't be retrofitted
Claim: The DMP and the participant information sheet are where sharing is effectively decided; participants who learned about open data late found their consent already precluded it, while those who plan early treat deposit as routine logistics.
Evidence: E-29, E-48, E-11, E-21, E-55
Sources: David, Bruce, Michelle, Stephen
Confidence: high
Counter-evidence: E-70 — Penni's case is client-driven mid-project, showing retrofitting is attempted in practice, with unresolved "how".
Opportunity: Intervene at DMP review and ethics application stage, not at deposit time.

## I-09 — The demand side is weak: reuse happens only when a form asks
Claim: Participants say they would reuse open qual data in principle, but in practice only look when a funding application requires a data-review, find little that's relevant or current, and default to primary collection.
Evidence: E-30, E-45, E-10, E-78, E-54
Sources: David, Bruce, Michelle, Penni, Stephen
Confidence: high
Counter-evidence: E-27 — David would reuse from a trusted repository; E-78 — Penni's homelessness example shows cyclical policy topics where decade-old data would help. Willingness exists; the archive is thin and reuse isn't normalised.
Opportunity: Surface reusable datasets at the point of proposal writing; build exemplar reuse cases to show what reuse looks like.

## I-10 — Sharing the analysis is more wanted, and more feasible, than sharing the data — but tools lock it in
Claim: Participants want to expose their "workings" (maps, codebooks, code frames, field diaries, debriefs) as the qualitative form of rigour, and see this as less ethically fraught than raw transcripts; NVivo project non-interoperability and a lack of shared vocabulary for analytic choices block it.
Evidence: E-17, E-18, E-46, E-53, E-69, E-76, E-77, E-07
Sources: David, Bruce, Stephen, Penni, Michelle
Confidence: medium-high
Counter-evidence: none found. Michelle's E-07 is about instruments rather than analysis, so is weaker support.
Opportunity: Standard for depositing analytical artefacts (codebooks, memos, framework matrices) independent of raw data; push CAQDAS vendors on export.

## I-11 — There is a shareability gradient, and participants draw the line in different places
Claim: Participants implicitly rank data types from freely shareable (instruments, protocols) through analytical artefacts, transcripts, to personal/AV data; they agree on the ordering but disagree on where "fine" ends.
Evidence: E-07, E-18, E-16, E-46, E-57
Sources: Michelle, David, Bruce, Stephen
Confidence: medium
Counter-evidence: E-51 — for Stephen the whole gradient collapses because even transcripts breach client confidentiality.
Opportunity: Let depositors choose per-artefact openness rather than a whole-dataset decision.

## I-12 — Participants hold four different theories of who moves the needle
Claim: There is no consensus on the lever: Bruce experiences funder mandate as decisive, David wants culture change via champions and distrusts mandates, Penni says only the commissioner/government can change it, Michelle says it depends on the participant. This is a tension, not a shared view.
Evidence: E-33, E-42, E-22, E-75, E-74, E-26, E-14
Sources: Bruce, David, Penni, Michelle
Confidence: medium
Counter-evidence: E-42 and E-22 are compatible if mandate is seen as necessary but not sufficient; Stephen (E-54) offers no lever at all.
Opportunity: Any intervention needs to address mandate, culture, and commissioner routes simultaneously rather than picking one.

## I-13 — Rich context layer as the workaround
Claim: Two participants independently propose the same fix for I-01: a narrative "here's what was going on" layer (README, field diary, recorded pre-briefs and debriefs) deposited alongside data.
Evidence: E-12, E-53, E-10
Sources: Michelle, Stephen
Confidence: medium
Counter-evidence: E-63 — Stephen doubts the primary researcher fully understands the place either, so the context layer can only ever be partial. Only two sources propose it explicitly.
Opportunity: Make a structured-but-free-text context document a standard deposit component.

## I-14 — People want a human conversation, not a form
Claim: The moments participants describe as reassuring involve talking to a person (library RDM staff, repository staff, an ethics panel); the moments they describe as alienating involve forms and portals ("little machines... sent off into the ether").
Evidence: E-13, E-11, E-20, E-73
Sources: Michelle, David, Penni
Confidence: medium
Counter-evidence: E-48 — Bruce is happy with a self-built spreadsheet process and never mentions wanting a conversation.
Opportunity: Offer a deposit consultation (a call with an archivist) as the default entry, especially for access-level classification.

## I-15 — "Open" in practice means findable and gated, not public
Claim: Even the strongest open-research advocate wants a password gate as reassurance; access levels, embargoes, and controlled repositories are what participants mean when they say they'd share.
Evidence: E-28, E-13, E-16, E-37, E-27
Sources: David, Michelle, Bruce
Confidence: medium
Counter-evidence: none found; Stephen and Penni don't address access tiers.
Opportunity: Lead communication with the safeguarded/controlled tiers; "open" as the headline term may be doing harm.

## I-16 — Career stage and structural position change the calculus
Claim: The cost of confidentiality and of openness falls differently by position: a PhD with a three-year clock can't absorb an unpublishable case; an ECR opens data partly to prove rigour; a contract researcher inherits others' DMPs; an emeritus can afford abstraction.
Evidence: E-56, E-23, E-32, E-01, E-64
Sources: Stephen, David, Bruce, Michelle, Penni
Confidence: medium
Counter-evidence: none found, but each position is represented by one person so this is more pattern-of-differences than shared claim.
Opportunity: Guidance and support differentiated by career stage rather than one-size-fits-all.

# Almost-insights (suspected, not supportable) — trap candidates

- **"Researchers want government/commissioners to lead on open qual data."** Penni only (E-74, E-75). Sounds general; is agency-specific. Academic participants locate the lever in funders, culture, or participants.
- **"Practitioners don't think about epistemology."** Penni only (E-66). No other participant is a practitioner.
- **"Commissioned qual data should be reclassified as national datasets."** Penni only (E-74). Novel and interesting, one voice.
- **"There should be public deliberation on acceptable anonymisation."** Penni only (E-72).
- **"Tiered consent menus are established good practice."** David only (E-25), with an unusual GDPR background (E-31); the interviewer explicitly says she hasn't heard it from anyone else.
- **"Mentioning archiving on the consent form deters participants."** Michelle only (E-02), and it's a fear, not an experience. Bruce's E-39 is about willingness to archive, not to participate, and is secondhand about Germany.
- **"Participant willingness to archive varies by country."** Bruce only, secondhand (E-39).
- **"Transcribing dialect for archive erases voice."** Bruce only (E-38). Plausibly generalisable, unsupported here.
- **"AI reuse of open qual data is a worry."** Stephen only (E-62).
- **"Everyone is in favour in principle."** Four are; Michelle actually says open data and qual "might not be compatible" (E-03) and frames herself as cautious (E-01). Smoothing her into "in favour with caveats" would be a misread.
- **"Library RDM support is the solution."** Michelle only rates it highly (E-11); David says he doesn't know where to start despite knowing the library exists (E-20).
- **"Funder mandates are the main driver."** Only Bruce experiences it that way (E-33); Michelle says technical barriers are low, Penni has no funder, Stephen never encountered a mandate.
- **"Qualitative researchers fear being scooped."** Stephen explicitly says the opposite for interpretivists (E-61); no one else raised IP.

# Log — changes of mind

- Expected consent/anonymisation to be the dominant theme. It is a theme (I-04, I-05), but context loss (I-01) and the quant-import objection (I-03) came up as often and with more heat. I-02 surprised me: nobody's epistemology is actually the blocker.
- Michelle and David are the same broad tradition (interpretive, participant-centred) and reach opposite conclusions on reinterpretation by strangers (E-04 vs E-19). I initially wanted to write "interpretivists welcome reinterpretation" as high confidence; Michelle forced me to add the counter-evidence and split relational from epistemic objections.
- Bruce holds two positions at once (E-35 "different readings are fine" and E-44 "outsiders miss what's going on"). I nearly filed these as contradictory; they're not — he's distinguishing legitimate difference from ignorance of context. That distinction became the spine of I-01.
- Penni generates a lot of quotable, general-sounding claims. Roughly a third of her evidence blocks ended up in the trap list. She's also the only one who connects data opacity to *analysis* opacity (E-69), which strengthened I-10 — so she's not just noise.
