## I-01 — Metadata cannot substitute for the lived context of data collection
**Claim:** Researchers across disciplines argue that standard metadata cannot convey the political, organizational, and temporal context needed to correctly interpret qualitative data, making context-loss a core epistemic barrier to responsible reuse.  
**Confidence:** medium  
**Sources:** bruce, michelle, stephen  
**Evidence:** michelle:0028, michelle:0032, stephen:0022, bruce:0019  
**Counter-evidence:** bruce:0035 — Bruce also frames divergent, context-poor readings by secondary researchers as legitimate and valuable rather than a loss, showing the field is split on whether context-loss is a problem to solve or an inevitable feature of reuse.  
**Opportunity:** Require a structured 'context statement' (political, organizational, and funding conditions at time of collection) as a mandatory repository field alongside standard technical metadata, rather than treating metadata as sufficient on its own.

**Evidence:** bruce:0023, penni:0031, stephen:0032

## I-05 — Consent obtained at collection cannot anticipate future, unknown uses of archived data

**Evidence:** michelle:0054, penni:0040  
**Counter-evidence:** david:0030 — David's proactive, staged consent process—explaining data flows upfront and revisiting them at each participant contact—offers a practical mitigation to this mismatch.  
**Opportunity:** Develop a 'living consent' model with periodic opt-in check-ins or granular consent tiers (research-use vs. archived-open vs. embargoed) rather than a single one-time consent statement covering unknown future use.
