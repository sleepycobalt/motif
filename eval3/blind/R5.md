## I-01 — Metadata cannot substitute for lived context, making reuse feel epistemically risky
**Claim:** Researchers argue that no amount of standard metadata can convey the political, organizational, and relational context that shaped qualitative data, so secondary analysts risk producing a 'sterile' or decontextualized understanding.  
**Confidence:** medium  
**Sources:** michelle, stephen  
**Evidence:** michelle:0028, stephen:0022  
**Counter-evidence:** bruce:0035 — Bruce reframes the same phenomenon (missing tacit context) as a legitimate feature of interpretive reading rather than a fatal loss of validity.  
**Opportunity:** Build flexible companion documentation formats (e.g., structured narrative 'Read Me' context files) into repository deposit workflows, rather than relying on fixed metadata spreadsheets, so depositors can capture temporal/political/organizational context.

## I-02 — Commercial and political sensitivity makes open access practically incompatible with some qualitative fieldwork
**Claim:** In fields where researchers depend on confidential access to organizations, individuals, or politically exposed participants, the terms under which access was granted make full data sharing practically unworkable regardless of researchers' openness in principle.  
**Confidence:** medium  
**Sources:** bruce, penni, stephen  
**Evidence:** stephen:0018, penni:0031, bruce:0023  
**Counter-evidence:** penni:0040, penni:0127 — Penni describes a politically sensitive public inquiry whose data is nonetheless destined for a national archive, reframing sensitivity as a solvable design problem driven by the inquiry's own transparency mission rather than an absolute bar to openness.  
**Opportunity:** Develop tiered/restricted-access and embargoed deposit templates specifically designed for commercially or politically sensitive qualitative data, paired with legal boilerplate that lets researchers negotiate partial disclosure rather than facing an all-or-nothing choice.

## I-03 — Full de-identification is often technically difficult, though some researchers see the barriers as primarily ethical rather than technical
**Claim:** Researchers report that genuine, complete de-identification of qualitative data is rarely achievable because contextual or tacit knowledge (accents, roles, organizational details) can re-identify individuals even after names and places are removed.  
**Confidence:** medium  
**Sources:** bruce, penni, stephen  
**Evidence:** bruce:0023, stephen:0032, penni:0031  
**Counter-evidence:** michelle:0048, david:0040 — Michelle argues that technical de-identification is not a major issue and locates the real barriers as ethical/relational, while David reframes identifiability not as a technical failure but as a matter negotiated directly with participants starting from a default of openness.  
**Opportunity:** Since perfect anonymization is often unachievable or contested as a framing, make tiered/restricted-access deposit categories (as used by UKDA) the default expectation rather than treating fully anonymized open release as the only acceptable goal.
